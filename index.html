UX case study

Amazon Prime Music

One million songs, free for Prime members. Designing a streaming music benefit inside the Amazon Digital Music Store, and learning what happens when speed to market shapes the experience.

Role Design lead, Digital Music Store
Timeline Sept 2020 – May 2021
Platforms Kindle Fire, Fire Phone, iOS, Android, desktop, web
Team 3 PMs, 1 lead designer, 7 platform designers

00 My Role

Intro and my role

I led the design of Prime Music for the Digital Music Store across Kindle Fire, Fire Phone, iOS, Android, desktop, and web, from the start of the project in September 2020 until May 2021. My focus was on customer pain points in browsing and discovery.

Customer insights & ideation

Worked with three project managers and one lead designer to uncover insights and turn concepts into features that addressed customer behaviors and motivations.

Experience strategy & vision

Built frameworks and prototypes to communicate the vision, design principles, and content strategy, to align teams and support decisions.

Planning & scope

With my PM colleagues, defined the product, advocated for customer-centric goals balanced against business objectives, and prioritized and negotiated features for launch and post-launch phases.

Oversight & coordination

Led design efforts and coordinated with seven platform designers and their PM counterparts to tailor features to each platform.

Design execution & validation

Executed designs for Kindle Fire, Android, iOS, and the iOS mobile Digital Music Store: user journeys, wireframes, prototypes, and design specifications.

Leadership

Presented designs to executives, senior stakeholders, and Amazon teams throughout the project to gain buy-in and keep alignment with strategy.

01 Design Problem

Design problem

Fostering stronger customer relationships

Since 2019, digital music download sales had steadily declined, with a significant shift toward on-demand streaming, which now represents a large share of the music industry's revenue. Music consumption was changing quickly, and that was a new challenge for Amazon.

Our task was to adapt and enter the highly competitive U.S. on-demand streaming market. Prime Music would offer a curated selection of one million songs and catalogue titles (at least six months old), available only to Prime members to stream and download. The goal was to build deeper, more meaningful relationships with Amazon customers.

Good, fast, cheap

To launch quickly, we were asked to design and build Prime Music inside the existing Digital Music Store and Music Library architecture. It was seen as the least risky option. The core assumption: millions of customers visit Amazon daily, so extending the familiar “acquire, play, manage” model would get the product to market faster and at lower cost.

That early architectural decision shaped the quality of the customer experience, both in what we could create and in how we could meet user expectations.

Chasing waterfalls

Work was split into parallel workstreams for the Music Library and the Digital Music Store; I led design for everything related to the store. Each feature phase started on the reference platform, Kindle Fire. Once a feature was designed and approved, engineering began building it, while I adapted it with platform designers and moved on to designing the next feature.

Working backward from a fixed launch date with aggressive scope meant design became part of an engineering-driven process. Sign-off milestones came from engineering estimates, and design got whatever time was left.

The core design questions

  1. How could one store satisfy both Prime members and customers buying music, when their motivations and goals are very different?
  2. How would we use existing design patterns that were heavily optimized for selling music?

02 Stakeholders

Stakeholders

The project touched every part of the music business, so I had to coordinate with, and get buy-in from, many teams, both co-located and distributed.

  • Product management: three project managers, plus a PM partner I sketched and white-boarded flows with.
  • Design: one lead designer, and seven platform designers with their PM counterparts.
  • Engineering: platform engineering teams whose estimates set sign-off milestones, and who built from my specs.
  • Quality assurance: teams who wrote test cases from the design documentation.
  • Leadership: executives, senior stakeholders, and senior leadership who approved designs.
  • Other teams: the Music Library workstream, and other teams outside music that the store approach relied on.
“Managing feedback was even more challenging and felt like a swinging pendulum of viewpoints.”

The team spent a disproportionate amount of time debating design decisions when data could not easily be gathered. The result was agony, paralysis, and growing skepticism about instinct in the design process. I noticed this early and invested in documentation that explained design rationale. It was time-consuming upfront, but it saved a lot of back-and-forth later. Design principles and a content prioritization framework made my decision-making visible and helped the team share the vision.

Prototypes, shared as videos, became the most effective way to get feedback from the team, consensus from stakeholders, and approval from senior leadership.

03 End-users

End-users

Embedding Prime Music in the Amazon Digital Music Store meant the experience had to work for everyone:

  • Prime members, who get Prime Music at no extra cost and can stream, download, and add Prime-eligible songs, albums, and playlists to their library.
  • Non-Prime customers buying music in the same store, whose goals are about purchase.
  • Existing music owners who had bought songs (on Amazon or elsewhere) that belonged to Prime-eligible albums, and could complete those albums from their library.

Research split listeners into two primary segments: lean-back listeners, who want music curated for them, and lean-forward listeners, who actively control and select what they hear. They used Kindle Fire, Fire Phone, iOS, Android, PC, Mac, and the web store on mobile and desktop.

The design principles also asked us to design for the whole customer lifecycle: Unaware, Interested, First-time, Regular, and Passionate.

04 Primary Research

Primary research

Hierarchical card sort (8 participants)

I hypothesized that Prime-eligible content should be prioritized differently when a customer does not have high purchase intent. With no qualitative data to support that, I ran a hierarchical card sort with eight participants to learn how customers think about categories of content and what matters most to them in the context of Prime.

In the think-aloud, participants ranked content by what felt predictable and trustworthy as relevant, then by what made them curious, and last by what they were skeptical about. Genre ranked first and Playlist second; Featured ranked last.

Large-scale playlist survey

We surveyed casual listeners about how they identify with and choose playlists:

  1. Genre and mood were the strongest influences when choosing music. Activity was also reported.
  2. Genre is used to find music that matches a mood, or to exclude certain types of music.
  3. Customers think about moods and genres in heavily overlapping ways.

Button usability test (16 participants)

Two button concepts, $0.00 and +Add, were tested with 16 participants (results under Design Decisions).

Extended beta and guerrilla testing

Two months before launch we ran an extended beta and guerrilla user testing. The top risks were: (1) playback quality, performance, and stability; (2) wayfinding; and (3) playing and collecting music. These were the same issues we heard after launch.

Post-launch diary study and usability testing

After launch I led a research plan to get deeper qualitative insight into pain points and opportunities. Over two weeks we ran a diary study and usability testing, and tested the viability of future concepts. I shared the learnings as a customer journey map.

05 Secondary Research

Secondary research

Planning drew on what the source calls extensive customer and market research. It does not say which findings came from which method or source, so they are grouped here.

  • Market shift: download sales declining since 2019, with on-demand streaming making up a large share of industry revenue.
  • Playlists go with activities: customers use playlists to match a mood or an activity, such as relaxing, working out, cleaning, or reading.
  • Songs remain the same: customers listen to only 19% of their own music library, which is mostly catalogue songs rather than albums.
  • Streaming to save: price sensitivity was what moved customers to on-demand streaming.
  • Collectors listen more: paid-tier customers invest more in building their libraries and stream four times as much.
  • Show me you know me: customers expect Amazon to know them and serve personalized recommendations.

I also drew on published methods: reversing the polarity of an assumption, from Marty Neumeier’s Metaskills: Five Talents for the Robotic Age, and the Think, Do, Feel exercise from Gamestorming.

06 Competitive Analysis

Competitive analysis

The source document has no feature-by-feature competitor comparison. What it does show is the market Prime Music was entering, and how the team positioned against it.

  • Positioning: the best-value music service for Amazon customers, not a “me-too” on-demand streaming service. Not an exhaustive catalog, but help finding music people will love from a selection they will actually listen to.
  • The Prime bundle: Prime Music joined two-day free shipping, unlimited streaming of popular movies and TV shows, more than 500,000 free ebooks, and unlimited photo storage.
  • In-house analogs: we assumed customers would follow the acquire-then-play path set by Prime Instant Video and the Kindle Lending Library. That assumption turned out to be wrong for music.
  • Customers’ mental model: the business did not see itself as a standalone streaming service, but customers did, and they judged the experience that way.
  • Threat named in the SWOT: clarity and aesthetics versus competitors.

07 User Personas

User personas

The source does not include formal personas. It describes customer segments and goals, summarized here.

Lean-back listener

Prefers a passive experience where music is curated for them. Maps to the playlist finding: “I don’t have to think. Someone else put it together for me.”

Lean-forward listener

Actively controls and selects what they listen to.

Collector

A paid-tier customer who invests in building a library and streams four times as much.

Prime member vs. music buyer

Prime members felt they had already paid for the music through their membership; purchasing customers came to the same store to buy.

The journey map listed customer goals by stage, including: “I want to find familiar music I love,” “I want to discover new music you think I’ll love,” “I want to type as little as possible and get to results as quickly as possible,” and “I want to quickly listen to music I’ve recently listened to.”

08 Design Decisions

Design decisions

Design principles

A set of nine principles made my decision-making visible to the team.

1. Content first: playlists plus genres

The card sort showed we needed to prioritize playlist content, and that genres were a familiar way to browse. I combined the two into the Prime Playlist browse experience, then paired the category ranking with feasibility scores to build the rest of the browse structure.

2. Dreams of the bargain bin

I imagined the worst possible perception of Prime Music: the bargain bin in a record store, discounted music nobody wanted. Listing its traits gave me the opposite principles for planning content. It became clear customers would get more value if we told a story behind the music. Campaign composites got the team excited about that future, even though many concepts weren’t feasible for launch.

3. Moody listening: one filter list

Because genre and mood overlap so much in customers’ minds, I designed Prime Playlist browse as a single filter list. It reduces upfront decisions, removes the need to switch between genre and mood, and carries a stronger information scent.

4. The Everything Store: “buy” Prime music for free

The Store-plus-Prime direction felt wrong from the start, but the decision was made. Instead of catastrophizing, I analyzed the approach’s strengths and modeled Prime Music on what customers already knew: discovering and buying music in Amazon’s store. We proposed letting Prime members purchase Prime-eligible music for free. The next challenge was showing which content was Prime-eligible and how to collect and play it.

5. The controversial blue button

This small button was among the team’s most debated decisions: what it said, what it looked like, and what it did. I started with the Think, Do, Feel exercise, working backward from the aspirational thoughts, feelings, and actions I was designing for, and narrowed to two concepts: $0.00 and +Add. Testing with 16 participants found:

  • Customers were delighted by the blue.
  • Customers could tell which content was Prime-eligible.
  • Customers did not understand +Add and thought it would charge them.
  • Customers thought selecting $0.00 meant they would own the music.

The ownership confusion surprised me and showed how much the store context shaped perception. Under pressure to move on, I went ahead with $0.00 because it would be easy to pivot if needed.

6. Last-minute pivots

Playback: beta customers said Prime streams took too long to start and often stopped. Performance and reliability were the product experience. A mini-hackathon fixed the critical issues.

Wayfinding: customers struggled to find Prime Music, felt lost, and could not re-find music they had collected. They didn’t expect to look for it in a store; “renting” music did not exist in their minds. We pulled the Prime sections out into their own area in the top-level navigation. It tested well for discoverability, but the items were really deep links into the store, so customers wandered into other store areas without knowing why. The team was divided on this band-aid, but accepted it for launch.

“Why can’t it be a standalone section? Why do I have to see buy buttons everywhere?” Anonymous beta participant

Playing and collecting: customers valued add-to-library but found it laborious and confusing. The team wanted to add playback to the store. I argued against it: stretching retail patterns optimized for selling would clutter the experience for everyone and ignore the complexity of playing, downloading, and managing music. Digging deeper, customers were also frustrated by seeing purchasable, non-Prime music. Our designs said buy, buy, buy, while customers felt they had already paid. A pure music experience needed a different strategy and many more months.

“It is cumbersome to play selected music. When you click on ‘add to library’ from the initial Amazon music screen, a user would logically think he/she could play the music. Unfortunately, that is not the case.” Anonymous beta participant

Prototype

Every feature phase went through requirements, consensus, approvals, detailed specs, and handoffs. I sketched and white-boarded concepts and flows with my PM partner, then went straight into high-fidelity comps. That was practical because I was working with many existing patterns.

Next I sliced the comps into a prototype. Early on, I prototyped only the highest-risk areas. Prototyping was the most effective way to get feedback from the team, consensus from stakeholders, and approval from senior leadership. I shared prototypes as videos and reused them for usability testing.

Once the team had a working prototype, we put it in customers’ hands. The extended beta and guerrilla testing two months before launch surfaced the top risks: playback, wayfinding, and playing and collecting music.

UI kit

11 Final Designs

Final designs

App designs for Kindle Fire and Android mobile, plus core screens from the service.

The service

Prime members can listen to and download from more than one million songs, hundreds of curated playlists, and personalized ad-free stations.

  • Turbocharge your library: add any Prime-eligible song, album, or playlist to stream, take offline, or mix with your own collection.
  • Complete your albums: if you own songs from a Prime-eligible album, find the remaining tracks from your library.
  • Playlists for every moment: Prime Playlists for moods and activities, built by Amazon’s music experts.

12 Outcome

Outcome

Launch

On the evening of June 11 we began rolling out the updated Prime Music to Kindle Fire, iOS, Android, PC, Mac, and the Amazon Digital Music Store on mobile and desktop. The launch went off without a hitch, which was remarkable given the scale and complexity of the deployment.

A not-so-fast follow

Post-launch feedback mirrored what usability and beta testing had already found, and there was no quick fix. With launch pressure gone, we had the space to do it right. The diary study and journey map built empathy across the team and became a breakthrough. After six months of design and development, we shipped the first of many updates: a Prime-only area optimized for Prime members and fully separate from the Digital Music Store. We had finally created the minimal desirable experience for customers.

What customers said

“Great update. You have fixed my one gripe about the app. Now I can stream without adding to my library. Thanks for listening to your customers.”
“Prime Music makes my Prime membership so much better! Amazon’s big data team did a great job understanding my music taste.”
“So many great songs. Think my Prime membership just paid for itself! Now I just have to get all my mobile devices loaded up!”
“I love this app! It frees up storage space on my phone, but allows me access to TONS of free music choices since I am a Prime member.”

What I learned

Your customers won’t forgive you because it was quicker and cheaper to build it that way. Bias for action turned into a bias for delivery. We measured outputs instead of outcomes, and let “how quickly can we build it?” define what we built. If we had asked “are we building the right thing?” as often as “will we meet our date?”, we would have launched a more reliable, intuitive, and polished product sooner. Customers should have defined viability before the technology and the date did.

Launching is only the beginning. At launch I knew where the usability issues and missing features were, and how much effort had gone to non-critical features at the expense of performance and reliability. My dissatisfaction wasn’t perfectionism but an insistence on quality, which is the responsibility of the whole organization. I’m proud of what I accomplished for Amazon: the launch exposed what was broken in the product and in how we worked, and left the team better placed to learn. As Amazon says, it’s still Day One.
